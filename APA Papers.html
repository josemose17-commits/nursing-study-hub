Paper patterns

Almost every nursing paper is an introduction, a few body sections and a conclusion. What changes is what the body sections do. Learn the pattern and any topic fits it.

The shape of every paper. The introduction is a funnel: start broad (why the topic matters), narrow to your focus, and end with the thesis or purpose statement. Each body section proves one part of that thesis. The conclusion is the funnel upside down: restate the thesis in new words, sum up, and end on why it matters. No new sources in the conclusion.

APA 7 student paper format

Page setup
  • 1-inch margins on all sides; US letter.
  • One font throughout: Times New Roman 12, or Calibri 11, Arial 11, Georgia 11.
  • Double-space everything, including the title page and references. No extra space before or after paragraphs.
  • Align left (ragged right edge); don’t justify.
  • Indent the first line of every paragraph 0.5 inch (use Tab or paragraph settings, not spaces).
  • Page number in the top right corner of every page, starting with 1 on the title page.
  • Student papers have no running head unless your instructor asks.

In-text citations

Cite every fact, number or idea that came from a source, even when you paraphrase. Each in-text citation matches an entry on the references page, and every reference is cited at least once.

Paraphrase, don’t quote

Nursing instructors want your own words. Read the passage, close it, write the idea in your words, then check you didn’t copy phrases. Still cite it. Keep quotes for exact definitions or striking statements, and use few of them.

Quotes under 40 words go in “quotation marks” with a page number. Quotes of 40 or more words are a block: new line, indented 0.5 inch, no quotation marks, citation after the final period.
How often to cite

Cite the first time you use a source in a paragraph. In a narrative citation, you can leave out the year in later sentences of the same paragraph. A new paragraph that uses the source again needs a new citation.

Patients with heart failure benefit from daily weights (Ignatavicius et al., 2024). [new paragraph] Daily weights also detect fluid gain early (Ignatavicius et al., 2024).
Privacy in reflection and case papers

Never name patients or meeting members, and use no initials, room numbers or birthdates (HIPAA). For AA, NA and Al-Anon, anonymity is a core tradition: describe what was shared without identifying who shared it. What a person told you is not cited as a source.

References page

Rules
  • New page, References bold and centered at the top.
  • Alphabetical by the first author’s last name; double-spaced; 0.5-inch hanging indent.
  • Up to 20 authors listed: last name, initials, with “&” before the last.
  • Article and book titles in sentence case (only the first word, the first word after a colon, and proper nouns capitalized). Journal names in Title Case.
  • Italics: book titles, webpage titles, journal names and volume numbers. Not article titles.
  • DOIs as links: https://doi.org/10.xxxx. No “Retrieved from.”

Writing it

  1. Read the rubric firstHighlight the required sections, page limit, number and type of sources, and due date. Turn the rubric’s sections into your headings so the grader finds each one.
  2. Find sources before writingUse the library databases (CINAHL, PubMed), not a general web search. Prefer peer-reviewed sources from the last 5 years, plus your textbook and organizations (CDC, WHO, ANA). Wikipedia, blogs and .com health sites are not scholarly.
  3. Write a thesis or purpose sentenceOne sentence that says what the paper will show. Every section must support it.
  4. Draft from the outlineUse the Word file: replace each highlighted note with your paragraph. Don’t polish yet. The body comes first; write the introduction and conclusion last.
  5. Revise for meaning, then for rulesRead it aloud. Check that every paragraph has a topic sentence and a citation, then work through the final checklist below.
The paragraph pattern (MEAL)
  • Main idea: a topic sentence that makes one claim.
  • Evidence: a paraphrased fact from a source, cited.
  • Analysis: your explanation of why it matters or how it connects (the part most students skip).
  • Link: a sentence that ties back to the thesis or leads to the next paragraph.
4–6 sentences, about 120–180 words. One idea per paragraph; a new idea starts a new paragraph.
Scholarly voice
  • No contractions (don’t → do not), slang, or questions to the reader.
  • Be specific: “three times a week” instead of “often.”
  • First person (“I”) is allowed in APA 7 for what you did and in reflections, but many nursing instructors don’t allow it in formal papers. Follow the rubric.
  • Avoid “I feel,” “I think,” “obviously,” “proves.” Research “suggests” or “indicates.”
Bias-free language
  • Person-first: “a person with schizophrenia,” “a patient with a substance use disorder,” not “a schizophrenic” or “an addict.”
  • Use the singular “they” when gender is unknown.
  • “Older adults,” not “the elderly.” Name racial and ethnic groups with capitals (Black, Hispanic or Latino, Asian).
Numbers and abbreviations
  • Words for zero to nine, numerals for 10 and up; but always numerals for ages, doses, percentages, times, scores and measurements (a 7-year-old, 5 mg, 3%).
  • Spell out a number that starts a sentence.
  • Define an abbreviation the first time: heart failure (HF). Then use only the abbreviation.
Transitions
  • Adding: furthermore, in addition, similarly.
  • Contrasting: however, in contrast, conversely.
  • Cause and effect: therefore, as a result, consequently.
  • Examples: for example, specifically.
  • Concluding: overall, in summary, ultimately.
Academic integrity

Your instructor and the plagiarism checker see copied phrasing, even when it’s cited. Write from your notes, not from the source. If you use an AI tool, check your course policy first and cite or disclose it as required. Never submit text you can’t explain.
