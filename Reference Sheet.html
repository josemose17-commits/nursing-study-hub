Therapeutic communication

What it is

Therapeutic communication is the specialized way nurses talk with patients to build a therapeutic (healing) relationship. It uses both words and body language, and it keeps the focus on the patient's needs and feelings, not the nurse's.

It came from nursing theorist Hildegard Peplau (Theory of Interpersonal Relations, 1950s). Use it in every patient encounter, not just with psych patients.

Why it matters in med-surg: anxious, scared or angry patients don't take in teaching, don't report symptoms and don't follow the plan. Good communication is how you get an accurate assessment and safe discharge teaching.

How to pick the answer on an exam

  • Pick the response that keeps the focus on the patient's feelings or concerns and invites them to say more (open-ended, reflecting, sharing an observation, offering self).
  • Rule out anything that is a "why" question, false reassurance ("don't worry", "everything will be fine"), advice ("if I were you…"), changing the subject, defending yourself or staff, judging or arguing.
  • If the patient is in physical danger (chest pain, can't breathe, suicidal), safety comes first. Act, then communicate.

Therapeutic techniques (use these)

Open-ended questionsQuestions that can't be answered yes/no, so the patient tells their story."Tell me what's been worrying you."
Active listeningShow interest with words and body language: eye contact, nodding, short prompts."Go on…"
Using silenceGive the patient time to think and process. Let them break the silence.(Nurse stays quiet and present while the patient gathers their thoughts.)
Reflecting / restatingRepeat back what the patient said so they hear their own words and know you're listening."You feel like no one is telling you what's going on."
ClarifyingAsk follow-up questions when the message is vague, so you both understand the same thing."Where exactly is the pain? Does it spread anywhere?"
Sharing observationsSay what you notice about the patient's appearance or mood."I noticed you're crying. Would you like to share what's making you sad?"
Sharing feelingsEncourage the patient to put their feelings into words; make it safe to do so."How are you feeling about the diagnosis?"
Sharing empathyShow you understand and share what the patient feels (not just feel sorry for them, which is sympathy)."That sounds really frightening."
Sharing hopeOffer a sense of possibility, but keep it factual. Being present and listening is a way to share hope."I'll stay with you, and we'll take this one step at a time."
Sharing humorOnly when the patient leads with it; often not appropriate.(Family shares a funny story; nurse listens and shares a related one.)
Using touchCan comfort, but some patients find it unsettling. Ask first and respect cultural norms."Is it okay if I hold your hand?"
Offering selfBeing present: sitting with the patient, spending time, with no strings attached."I'll sit with you for a while."
Asking relevant questionsGather what you need before helping the patient decide, without overwhelming them."How long were you in the hospital last time, and what was it for?"
Providing informationTeach at every encounter and invite questions."This is your metoprolol. It slows your heart rate. What questions do you have?"
FocusingHelp the patient pick one concern or goal to work on."You mentioned several worries. Which one matters most to you right now?"

Nontherapeutic techniques (avoid these)

Interrogating ("why" questions)Makes the patient defensive and they stop talking."Why didn't you take your medication like I told you?"Better: "Tell me what made it hard to take your medication."
False reassuranceComfort that isn't based on facts. It brushes off the patient's fear."Don't worry, everything will be fine."Better: let them voice the fear, then offer realistic hope.
Giving adviceYour personal opinion takes away the patient's own decision."If it were me, I wouldn't see that provider."Better: give facts and help them weigh their options.
Changing the subjectTells the patient their concern doesn't matter.Patient talks about chest pain; nurse asks, "What insurance do you have?"Better: stay on the patient's topic.
Automatic responsesReady-made phrases said without thought (clichés, stereotypes, empty promises)."I'll be right back" (and never comes back).Better: be specific and follow through.
Judgmental responsesApproving or disapproving puts the nurse above the patient."You have lung cancer because you smoked for 40 years."
Defensive responsesDefending yourself, staff or the hospital instead of hearing the complaint."I didn't do anything!" / "We're just short-staffed."Better: listen for the deeper concern behind the criticism.
Arguing / challengingImplies the patient is lying or wrong, and destroys trust.Patient raises their voice about the diet; nurse raises theirs too.
AttackingCriticizing, dominating or manipulating. If the patient felt attacked, apologize and rebuild."You came in here drunk and started hitting people."
Passive or aggressive responsesAvoiding the issue, or provoking a fight. Use assertive communication instead.Patient apologizes for soiling the bed again; nurse says, "Yeah, yeah, I know."
Not listening / not presentBeing distracted, like scrolling on a phone during the encounter.(Nurse charts and doesn't look up while the patient talks.)

Body language (nonverbal)

  • Keep words and body language congruent (matching). If they don't match, the listener believes the nonverbal message.
  • Eye contact reads as honest and respectful, especially with bad news. In some cultures it's disrespectful, so ask about the patient's preferences.
  • Head nodding shows acceptance. Touch can comfort (and helps with a blind patient), but respect boundaries and culture.

Checking that it worked: the feedback loop

  1. Reflect on the response. Restate what the patient said, so they know you heard them and keep talking.
  2. Compare the response to your goal. If what the patient says or does matches what you meant to get across, it worked.
  3. Revise the message if it didn't work, then check again. Keep going until the patient can repeat the instructions back.

After teaching, check learning with teach-back (patient restates it in their own words), return demonstration (patient shows you, e.g. drawing up insulin) or questions. "Do you understand?" is not enough: patients often say yes.

Then document: what you taught, how, the patient's response, materials given, any barriers, and what still needs teaching.

Interpreters

  • Use a certified medical interpreter (National Board of Certification for Medical Interpreters).
  • Not family: they may not know medical terms and can mistranslate. Not other staff: it can violate the patient's privacy.
  • Hospitals that take federal money (Medicare, Medicaid) must provide one (Title VI of the Civil Rights Act).

Barriers to communication

PhysicalNoise, alarms, no privacy (thin walls), time pressure, distance, broken equipment.Fix: close the door, silence alarms, find a private space, follow through on time.
EmotionalFear (doesn't hear you because of what's coming), pride (won't ask for help, then falls), anger.Fix: address the emotion first, then teach. De-escalate anger.
PerceptualPast experiences, beliefs, triggers (e.g. a trauma survivor who feels dominated), and the nurse's own biases and stereotypes.Fix: self-awareness; manage expectations ("you'll still have pain after knee surgery").
CulturalDifferent norms for eye contact, touch, family roles.Fix: cultural competence (understanding the patient's context) plus cultural humility (ongoing self-reflection).

Emotional intelligence (controlling and expressing emotions well) helps you get past barriers: self-awareness, self-management, social awareness and relationship management.

For sensory barriers, ask the patient how they prefer to learn (large print, Braille, audio, electronic) before teaching.

Motivational interviewing (MI)

A counseling approach for behavior change (quitting smoking, low-sodium diet, taking meds). Show empathy, point out the gap between what the patient does now and what they want, and accept that ambivalence (mixed feelings) is normal.

  1. Engaging: build the relationship with active listening and nonverbal cues.
  2. Focusing: pick a clear direction and goal together.
  3. Evoking: draw out the patient's own reasons to change.
  4. Planning: small, achievable SMART goals (Specific, Measurable, Attainable, Relevant, Timely).

Professional communication

  • Empathy vs sympathy: sympathy is feeling sorry for someone; empathy is understanding and sharing their feelings while treating them as able to make their own decisions.
  • Assertiveness: confident and respectful. Speak up for patients who can't (unconscious, nonverbal, no advocate) until the concern is addressed.
  • Courtesy and names: introduce yourself, verify identity, and call patients what they prefer ("Please call me Tom").
