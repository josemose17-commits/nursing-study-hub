In the hospital: how a drug gets from the pharmacy to your patient
Order → pharmacist → eMAR. The provider orders it, a pharmacist checks it, and it appears on the electronic medication administration record with the time, dose and any hold parameters.
Automated dispensing cabinet (Pyxis, Omnicell). You log in with a fingerprint or badge, pick the patient, and only that drug’s drawer or pocket opens. Opioids and other controlled drugs need a count, and any unused part is wasted with a witness.
Unit dose. Each pill is sealed in its own labeled packet with a barcode. Leave it sealed until you are at the bedside.
Barcode scanning. Scan the patient’s wristband, then every medication. It catches the wrong patient, drug or dose, but it does not replace your own checks.
IV bags and pumps. Small “piggyback” bags (antibiotics, KCl) and continuous drips run on smart pumps with a drug library. High-alert drugs (heparin, insulin, opioids, potassium, magnesium, chemotherapy) need an independent double-check.
Crash cart. Prefilled emergency syringes (epinephrine, atropine, amiodarone, adenosine and others), sealed and checked daily.
Refrigerator. Insulin, epoetin, filgrastim, dornase, protamine, vaccines and some IV drugs live in the med-room fridge.
Generic names on everything. Hospitals stock generics, so the packet usually says “metoprolol tartrate”, not “Lopressor”. Learn both names. Pill color changes with the manufacturer, so never identify a pill by color alone; warfarin’s strength colors are the exception.
